# Source reliability

Read this when an argument depends mainly on what someone said, wrote, or reported (a claim from an expert, a news item, a user's secondhand account, a document), and you need to decide how much weight it deserves.

Nyāya weighs testimony the way it weighs any other evidence. A statement counts when it comes from a reliable speaker and hangs together as a statement.

## The speaker

- Would they know? Look for direct access, relevant expertise, or a record of being right on this kind of question.
- Do they have a reason to mislead or to overstate, such as a financial interest, a position to defend, or a wish to please?
- Are they reporting what they saw, or what someone else told them?

## The statement

Nyāya names three conditions for a statement to convey a reliable meaning:

1. **Expectancy (ākāṅkṣā):** the parts depend on each other, so the statement forms one connected claim and not a list of unrelated items. "Cow, horse, man, elephant" tells you nothing.
2. **Compatibility (yogyatā):** the claim does not contradict itself or what is plainly possible. "He should sprinkle with fire" fails here.
3. **Proximity (sannidhi):** the parts belong together in one context, with nothing missing or taken out of place. A sentence pieced together from fragments said at different times is unreliable.

## How to use this

- Name which condition fails when a source looks weak ("the speaker has no direct access," "the claim contradicts itself," "this quote is out of context").
- Treat a strong argument resting on a weak source as weak.
- Where possible, find a second, independent source. Two sources that both trace back to the same original count as one.
- Say plainly what you could not verify.
